From a historical overview of cosmology to understanding what are X-ray faint galaxy clusters

The goal of these four small lectures is to allow non-experts to reach a level of knowledge to appreciate and understand a cutting-edge astrophysics research topic.

To do so we will need to start from the basics of cosmology: I will present an historical overview of how the scientific community understood how galaxy forms; why we observe that galaxies are not randomly sparse in the sky but are found in groups (so called galaxy clusters); and why galaxy clusters emit radiation in various wavelength (from optical to X-ray); we will see how scientists use this information to gain more and more information on the underlying cosmology of our Universe.

We we will also review the theoretical counterpart of observations: numerical simulations as a tool to interpret the large amount of observed data.

Finally, we will have all basic knowledge to appreciate and understand one particular cutting edge scientific topic: why tracking the origin of so-called X-ray faint galaxy clusters can help advance future cosmology studies.

Recap of lecture I: historical overview

The first lecture of the serie will review the basic ingredients from modern cosmology study to the formation of galaxy clusters as well as why galaxies do cluster together in what we call galaxy clusters: they are the most massive and gravitationally bounded structures in our Universe. They have gigantic radii of order of ~1 mega parsec (Mpc); we will learn why they do emit both optical light and X-ray radiation; how to interpret these observations using theoretical models as numerical simulations and how to extract informations on the underlying cosmology governing our Universe by counting galaxy clusters in the sky.

This is a very complicated topic and from a non-expert point of view, it may seems like a magic how the astrophysics community reached such detailed knowledge by just observing it all from our planet Earth. However, astrophysicists did not build this huge knowledge budget in a linear and easy way: it took more than a century to slowly disentangle all the puzzling observations and possible solutions. Therefore the first lecture is strucutred with an historic approach: so to understand how humanity managed to slowly progress on such a complex topic.

How do we observe galaxy clusters?

One may naively think that galaxies in our Universe are sparse and randomly distributed. However, take this recent image from the Euclid telescope where each dot here is a very distant galaxy: if we look at the central part of the image we can see that the dots are clumped together. In fact, in general, galaxies in the Universe can be found in groups and clusters of galaxies because of gravitational attraction.

The study of these so called galaxy clusters is fundamental for studying cosmology and within the last century we developed the theoretical framework to understand why they cluster and to predict (on average) how many clusters we would count based on different details of the underlying cosmology of our Universe. Finding these clusters is not an easy task, and we also need also sophisticated algorithms to recognize galaxy clusters in large images (e.g. AMICO by Bellagamba et al. 2017).

Recap of lecture II and III: numerical simulations

To better interpret the images and the expectations, people also use the so-called numerical simulations. In the second lecture we will explore this tool: we will see how these computer simulations include a primordial almost-homogeneus distribution; we will see how simulators model the "ingredients" of the universe as the gas, the star, and the dark matter to follow their evolution with a resolution of single galaxies and volumes of cosmological scales (this requires hundreds of TB of RAM and physical storage, and hudreds of computers working in parallel in the so called computing clusters).

Simulations allow us to interpret and to guide observations. Just as small example, the average matter density of our Universe has some uncertainty as different observational surveys recover relateively different values (see the distribution of Ωm int this report from the Dark Energy Spectorscopic Instrument, DESI). Simulations can help solve this issue: one can execute a lot of numerical simulations, each with a different amount of matter in the simulated Universe, and then analyse all these simulations in search for signatures that observers can look for that are left by lower or higher values of average matter density (e.g. produce less or more galaxy clusters respectively).

To interpret better these discrepancies one need simulations that are able to reproduce extremely well the stellar and gas distribution inside galaxy clusters, so to predict respectively their optical and X-ray emission. Therefore in the third lecture we will review how numerical simulations can account for gas that forms stars, stars that can explode and enrich the surrounding gas with new elements, how they model the formation of super massive black holes and how they model the power ful jets that they can emit.

Recap of lecture IV: X-ray faint galaxy clusters

Finally, on the fourth lecture we will go to a cutting edges topics in astrophysics: a small piece of the much bigger puzzle of constraining the cosmology of our Universe. In particular we will study why the X-ray emission of some clusters are very faint.

This is important because number count of galaxy clusters are difficult and observational studies do model the possible missing X-ray faint objects. Therefore it is also important to know what kind of objects they are missing.

Numerical simulations are a wonderful tool to solve these problems: there we can search for clusters and dissect their gas and stellar components, we can track them in time or re-simulated them with different physics (e.g. with or without the formation of stars or of super massive black holes) to understand their imprint on galaxy cluster properties. The analyses in our simulations show a compelling hypothesis: that X-ray faint clusters tend to be old. The link would be because, in simulations the central bleck hole depleted the cluster from gas. While a black hole is negligibly tiny compared to a galaxy cluster, it can still manage to deplete the gas and to expel it from the galaxy cluster core.

The image here on the left is an overlay of various galaxy cluster observations of visible light from the Hubble Telescope (the galaxy cluster is so distant that each yellow dot is a galaxy like our Milky Way), and X-ray from Chandra telescope (in red-blue), see how the gigantic red jet can be so huge that it surpass the size of many galaxies and as it start from the center of the cluster, there are situations like the one in the picture where it can reach the very end of it: that is how much powerful super massive black hole jets can be.

Simulations also hints that old galaxy clusters tend to be morphologically more round as opposed to young clusters (they just formed and it takes time for the gas to thermalise). In conclusion, characterising the faint end of the X-ray clusters help observational studies to better model these poorly resolved or missing objsects.
